California gives families a public way to review licensing information before choosing child care. It only takes a few minutes, and it is worth doing for every licensed provider you are seriously considering, including me.

## Start with the facility search

The California Department of Social Services maintains the official Community Care Licensing facility search:

**[Search for a licensed facility](https://www.ccld.dss.ca.gov/carefacilitysearch/)**

If you are considering a small family child care home, ask for its nine-digit facility number. California notes that small homes generally cannot be found through a broad area search; you need the exact license number.

T.L.C. Footprints' facility number is [**394501929**](https://www.ccld.dss.ca.gov/carefacilitysearch/FacDetail/394501929).

## What the online record can show

Depending on the facility and its history, the record may include:

**License status and type.** Confirm that the license is active and that the facility type matches what the provider describes.

**Licensed capacity.** This is the maximum number authorized by the license, not necessarily the number present when your child attends.

**Inspection and visit reports.** Open the reports and read the details rather than relying only on the number of visits.

**Violations and citations.** Look at what happened, how serious it was, what correction was required, and whether the issue appears repeatedly.

**Complaint investigation reports.** When available, these provide more context than a complaint count alone.

## Know the database's limits

California says the online facility search contains five years of information. For older history or additional details, contact the regional licensing office shown on the facility's record.

The state also advises families not to rely on the database alone. Review the record, visit the program, ask to see current licensing information, and ask the provider about anything you do not understand.

## How to read a citation fairly

A citation deserves attention, but its presence alone does not tell the whole story.

Consider four things:

1. **What happened?** Read the report itself.
2. **Was anyone's health or safety at risk?** Distinguish an immediate safety concern from a documentation problem.
3. **Was it corrected?** Look for the plan of correction and follow-up information.
4. **Is there a pattern?** A repeated issue matters differently from an isolated issue that was promptly corrected.

Bring your questions to the tour. A thoughtful explanation of what happened, what changed, and how the provider prevents a repeat can give you useful context.

## A note about newer providers

A newly licensed provider may have little inspection history online. That is not automatically a concern, but it means there is less history available to help you evaluate the program.

Give more weight to the in-person visit, current licensing documents, references that families have agreed to provide, and the provider's answers about safety, supervision, communication, and daily care.

## What licensing cannot tell you

A license confirms that a provider is subject to California's requirements and oversight. It cannot tell you whether the environment feels warm, whether communication suits your family, or whether your child will connect with the caregiver.

Use licensing information as one part of the decision: check the record, ask direct questions, and visit in person.
