Starting child care is a meaningful change for both children and parents. Some children settle quickly, some need several weeks, and many have a mix of easy and difficult days.

There is no single correct timeline. Knowing what may happen can help you respond calmly without treating every hard morning as evidence that something is wrong.

## Adjustment is rarely a straight line

A first day may go smoothly because everything is new and interesting. A later drop-off may be harder once your child understands that child care is now part of the regular routine.

You may also notice changes at home: more tiredness, extra closeness, stronger emotions, or temporary changes in sleep and appetite. These can happen during a transition, but they should be discussed with your provider if they are intense, persistent, or concerning to you.

Progress can look uneven. One difficult day does not erase the comfortable moments that came before it.

## A calm drop-off routine

### Keep the goodbye warm and clear

Tell your child that you are leaving and that you will return. Slipping away unnoticed can make it harder for a child to trust the transition.

### Use a short, familiar phrase

Simple words are easier to repeat consistently: “I love you. You are safe. I will be back after snack.” Choose a promise you know you can keep.

### Let the caregiver take over

Once you have said goodbye, give the provider room to comfort and engage your child. Lingering can sometimes extend the uncertainty.

### Agree on an update plan

Before the first day, ask when the provider will contact you and whether a brief update after drop-off is realistic. Knowing the plan can make it easier to leave without repeatedly checking in.

## What can help before and after care

**Talk about the change simply.** Tell your child what will happen in concrete terms: who will be there, what they may do, and who will return for pickup.

**Keep home routines steady where you can.** Familiar meals, bedtime, and family rituals can provide reassurance while one part of the day is changing.

**Ask about comfort items.** A photo or familiar object may help if the provider allows it. For infants, soft comfort items never enter the sleep space.

**Choose the starting plan together.** Some children benefit from shorter initial days; others do better with a consistent schedule from the beginning. Ask what the provider can offer and what is likely to work for your child.

**Leave extra space after pickup.** Your child may need food, closeness, quiet play, or an earlier bedtime after managing a full day of new experiences.

## What parents may feel

Parents have a transition too. You may feel relieved, guilty, distracted, proud, or all four in the same morning.

Those feelings do not mean you chose incorrectly. Give yourself time to build trust with the caregiver and learn the new rhythm. Ask for the information you need, while also agreeing on communication that the provider can deliver consistently during a busy care day.

## When to start a deeper conversation

Bring concerns to your provider when you notice:

- Distress that remains intense or does not begin to improve
- A child becoming unusually withdrawn
- Fear focused on a particular person, place, or event
- Significant changes in eating, sleeping, or behavior
- A drop-off or pickup pattern that feels different from ordinary separation difficulty

Describe what you see without assuming the cause, and ask what the provider observes during the day. A useful conversation looks for patterns and a plan rather than trying to assign blame.

If you are worried about your child's health, development, or emotional well-being, contact the appropriate pediatric or child-development professional as well.
